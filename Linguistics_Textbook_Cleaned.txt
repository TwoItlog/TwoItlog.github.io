Getting Started

Language is an essential part of what it means to be human. We use language

to communicate, listen, read, and write. Language helps us build communities

and relationships, allowing us to bond and grow. Linguistics is a study of all

things related to language - how humans speak, the underlying structure of

language, how children acquire it, how it changes over time, and so on.


In this chapter, we are going to talk about the most basic concepts that you

need to know in this course. Let’s start with the following question:



  What does it mean to know a language?

When you are a native speaker of a language,


    1. You know the sounds of that language. You know which sounds do and

     do not exist in that language. You also know which sound combinations

      are or are not possible in your language.

2. Moreover, you are aware of words and word combinations and whether

      certain combinations of words are or are not possible.

    3. Additionally, you know which strings of words are meaningful

      constructions, and which are not. Let’s consider the following

      sentences:


    (1)   a. The man has seen the dog. (Grammatical and meaningful)

       * b. Seen man the has dog the.

          (Ungrammatical since it does not follow certain principles (rules) of

the English language)


Note: the asterisk (*) placed in front of a sentence signals that it is not correct

or grammatical.


As a native speaker of a language, you know whether a sentence is ambiguous

or not. For example, any native speaker of English knows that the following

sentence is ambiguous, and therefore has two meanings:


    (2) The tourist saw the astronomer with the binoculars.


Meaning 1: The tourist used the binoculars to see the astronomer.


Meaning 2: The astronomer that the binoculars saw had a telescope


Note that this linguistic knowledge is unconscious that is people speaking a

specific language are able to explain what constructions are correct or

incorrect in that language, but they are not able to explain why. This

unconscious knowledge is also called competence.

When you know a language, you also have the knowledge of the real-world

usage. You can make a distinction between familiarity and formality. For

example, you are aware that the sentence in (3a) is used in an informal context

while the sentence in (3b) is used in a formal context:


    (3)  a. What y’all watchin?

        b. What are all of you watching?


The above sentences are examples of register/style. The first one indicates an

informal register while the second one is an example of formal register.

Register/style refers to the levels of formality. Register is a variety of language

used in a particular social setting. Now, consider the following sentences:


    (4)  a. Gimme that, k?

        b. Could you give me that?

         c. Might I trouble you to give me that, if you’d be so kind?


Can you tell the difference between sentence (4a) and (4c)? In what context do

you use the first sentence? In what contexts do you use the other two?

You are right! All of these sentences are grammatical, but they differ in terms of

how formal they are. The first one is used in an informal context, probably with

your friends and close family members, and the last one is used in more formal

contexts. Can you name a few formal contexts in which you may use more

formal language?

It’s not just the informal register that is restricted to certain situations; it can be

equally inappropriate to use a formal register in casual situations. For example,

sentence (4c) would be a really strange thing to say if you were asking your

best friend to hand you a pen they are holding. Remember register is not about

grammaticality, but about appropriateness.


Do not forget that knowing a language also means being able to speak and be

understood by others who know that language.

Grammar

Grammar has various distinct meanings. For linguists, grammar is a mental

system of rules that exists in the head of native speakers. This system of rules

allows native speakers of a language to produce and understand sentences

that have never been produced or heard before. This is what we also call

mental grammar. Our mental grammar consists of 5 main components:


    1. Phonetics - deals with the perception and articulation of speech

     sounds

2. Phonology - deals with sound combinations (which sound

     combinations are possible)

    3. Morphology - deals with the structure of words

    4. Syntax - deals with sentence structure

    5. Semantics - deals with meaning and the interpretation of sentences


Therefore, grammar is a complex system of rules that governs how speakers

organize sounds into words and words into sentences.

Now that you know what we mean by ‘grammar’, you need to know that there

are two different approaches to study grammar: descriptive versus

prescriptive approach.

Prescriptive Grammar

Prescriptive Grammar (traditional grammar): Prescriptive grammarians

believe in absolute standard of correctness. This approach to grammar

governs the version of English considered appropriate for use by educated

speakers. Here we are dealing with should(s) and shouldn’t(s)- how you should

speak and how you shouldn’t speak.


 In prescriptive grammar:


a. One form is more logical than another

b. An appeal to classical forms

c. A preference for older forms of the language

d. Injunction against the use of foreign words (words borrowed from other

languages)


According to this view, language is either correct or incorrect and any educated

person should be able to understand at once and follow faithfully the norms of

correctness which are thought to be preserved in authorities’ reference works

such as dictionaries and handbooks.

Consider the following example:


7 ) Me and John went to the movies.

The answer is NO! From a prescriptive point of view, this sentence “should” be

‘John and I went to the movies.’

Note that in this course, we are NOT concerned with prescriptive grammar.

Instead, we make appeal to the concept of Descriptive Grammar, but what is

Descriptive Grammar?

Descriptive Grammar

Descriptive Grammar: A descriptive approach is one which studies and

characterizes the actual language of specific groups of people in a range of

situations. It does not bring any preconceived notions of “correctness” to the

task or favors the language of one social group as somehow better than those

of others. It describes how our grammatical system operates. It also aims at

recoding facts, describing the actual language (descriptive rules) as

comprehensively as possible, and avoids law-giving tone of school room

traditional grammars. Linguists are descriptive grammarians. When Linguists

write a grammar of a language, they attempt to describe the rules that govern

the grouping of the words of that language into meaningful patterns.


Descriptive Linguists counter the above prescriptive claims with the following:


a. All varieties of a language are valid systems with their own logic and

conventions.

b. There is no strong reason to expect one language to match the mold of

another.

c. Languages are continually changing in subtle ways without reducing their

usefulness, preciseness or aesthetic value.

d. All languages have adopted words from other sources.


Note that both approaches to language studies involve rules, but prescriptive

rules are about mandating language and descriptive rules are about describing

language (i.e., descriptive rules tell us what speakers do and don’t do,

prescriptive rules tell us what speakers should and shouldn’t do). Descriptive

rules form the basis for grammaticality judgments (i.e., the opinion of a native

speaker as to whether a given construction is well formed or not), which are

always made relative to a particular dialect (e.g., something that is

grammatical in Standard British English won’t necessarily be grammatical in

Standard Canadian English or African-American Vernacular English, etc.).


Below are some examples of prescriptive rules: a. Don’t end a sentence with a

preposition i. *“What are you looking at?” vs. “At what are you looking?” ii.

*“Who are you talking to?” vs. “To whom are you talking?” b. Don’t split an

infinitive (to+base form) i. *“To gradually understand the facts” ii. * “He seems

to really like it” c. Don’t use double negatives. Two negatives make a positive i.

“I didn’t see nobody” = I saw somebody. ii. “I haven’t got nothing”= I got

something.


And descriptive rules: a. Articles like the and a go before the noun. b. In English,

adjectives come before nouns to modify them. c. The word order in English is

Subject+Verb+Object. How do you feel about the two sentences below? Which

one violates descriptive rules? Which one violates prescriptive rules? 5) This is

the man you took a picture of. 6) Lucy saw myself last week.

Characteristics of Grammar

There are five characteristics of grammar:


1.Generality: all languages/dialects (and speakers/signers) have a grammar.

However, individual grammatical systems differ in the use of structure

formation rules. In Warlpiri (an indigenous language of Australia) a sentence

like: The two dogs now see several kangaroos could be translated by the

equivalent of any of the following sentences:


a. Dogs two now see kangaroos several.

b. See now dogs two kangaroos several.

c. See now kangaroos several dogs two.

d. Kangaroos several now dogs two see.

e. Kangaroos several now see dogs two.


2.Parity: all grammars are equal. -There is no such thing as a “primitive”

language. There is no such thing as a “good grammar” or “bad grammar”. All

grammars (unconsciously) instruct people on how to form and interpret words

and sentences of their language.


3.Mutability: grammars change over time Moreover, when grammars change,

they do so within strict limits. Also, it cannot be the case that that older

varieties of language are better than the new ones. There are no grounds to

accept the hypothesis that “languages attain a state of perfection at some

point in their history and that subsequent changes lead to deterioration and

corruption”.


4.Inaccessibility: grammatical knowledge is unconscious it cannot be figured

out just by thinking about it. Recall the examples from linguistic competence!


5.Universality: all grammars are alike in basic ways in spite of apparent

distinctions, all languages share an inventory of principles and properties

(including the so-called universals). Some examples include:


a. All languages use a small set of contrastive sounds to help distinguish words

from each other (to as opposed to do)

    b. any language that has a b sound, almost certainly has a p sound.

Standard Versus Non-standard

There are many varieties of English, each group follows a slightly different

subset of rules by which language operates. Standard language is the variety of

a language spoken by the most powerful group in a community and generally

held to be “correct” by prescriptive grammarians. Standard or prestige varieties

are usually based on the speech of the rich, powerful, and highly educated

members of a society (political elite or upper-class). People considered

standard speakers as long as they do not use any strongly stigmatized forms,

for example, I seen it instead of I have seen it or I ain’t got nothing instead of I do

not have anything or I have went instead of I have gone.

Keep in mind that non-standard/non-standard language does NOT mean

ungrammatical! A sentence can be informal and still grammatical. To show an

ungrammatical sentence we use the asterisk (*) before the sentence. A

sentence that is judged as ungrammatical in one dialect or variety can be

judged as grammatical and acceptable in another variety. Something that is

grammatical in Standard British English won’t necessarily be grammatical in

Standard Canadian English or African-American Vernacular English.

1. Introduction

In future chapters, we will examine the smallest units of language – phonemes.

Phonemes correspond to sounds and are considered building blocks of

language. However, on their own phonemes carry no significant meaning but

words do.


Morphology, a division of linguistics, looks at words and their formation. In

this chapter we will look at how morphology deals with categorization of

words, internal word structures, and processes by which a language creates

and modifies words.


We all agree that language is creative, and that humans have the ability to

produce and understand an infinite range of sentences and words never heard

before. In our mental lexicon, we store sounds from which we create words.

Simple words are also stored in our lexicon. Sounds and words, as well as

other signs, are forms to which meanings are attached.


The relation form-meaning is arbitrary (Saussure 1916); it is not iconic (based

on intrinsic properties). An important question: are all sounds and words we

store in our mental lexicon the same? We’ve seen that not all sounds possible

in human language are identical.


In the following sections, we are going to discuss the following:


    1. What is Morphology?

    2. Word Classes/Categories

3. Affixation

    4. Word-Formation Processes


This chapter, as well as the following, is dedicated to the analysis of the

structure (constituency) of words, the part of linguistics known under the name

of morphology. Morphology is from Greek morphos (‘form’) and logos

(‘science’). In linguistics, the study of the structure of words and the

component of grammar that includes the rules of word formation or how units

of meaning combine to form words.

2. Words and Their Structure

Have you ever had the opportunity to listen to a conversation in a foreign

language only to discover that you could not tell one word apart from the

other? As speakers of English, or any other languages, we have the ability to

segment sounds into meaningful words. So, what exactly does it mean to be a

word?


What is a word? An element that does not have to occur in a fixed position with

respect to neighboring elements. Consider the following sentence:


    (1) Elephants are amazing.


We have the intuition that the forms elephants is a word, and that the element

–s is not - -s never occurs in isolation in English and has to be attached to the

noun to which it belongs


    (2) *Elephant are-s amazing.

       *Elephant are amazing-s.


The intuition is that elephants is a word because it can occur in isolation, and

in different positions in the sentence.

(3) I like elephants.

    (4) Everyone at the zoo is crazy about elephants.

    (5) Many elephants live in Africa, but there are no elephants in Europe.


Moreover, the elephants example above demonstrates other important facts

about human language: - words are not unstructured - they are not the

smallest units of meaning The addition of –s is not insignificant.


A word, in linguistics, is the smallest free form. A free form can stand on its

own, without having to be attached to any other element. For instance:


(1) I have the laziest cat in the world.


We can see that laziest is a word in a sentence such as (1) but -est is not

because it is not a free form. We cannot put -est separately from lazy as

exemplified by (2). (Note that if an element cannot stand on its own, it is

written with a hyphen; an asterisk specifies ungrammaticality.)


(2) *I have -est the lazy cat in the world.


2.1. Morphemes


As we have seen in previous sections, words have an internal structure of

smaller units of meaning.


A morpheme is the smallest unit of meaning in a language, carrying some

information about either function or the meaning. Let’s start with a test. How

many elements (morphemes) do you think exist in the word ‘unhappiness’?


Correct! There are three words in this word: un-, happy-, and -ness. We say that

there are three morphemes in this word. These morphemes are meaningful

and cannot be broken down anymore. For examples, the morpheme un-

means ‘not’ and it cannot be further analyzed.

How about the word desk? How many morphemes are present in the word

‘desk’? Yes, the word ‘desk’ contains one morpheme, and as a result we call it a

simple word. However, a word such as ‘happiness’ is called complex since it

consists of more than one morpheme. Therefore, Words may be made up of a

single morpheme (jump) which cannot be further analysed into smaller

morphemes. These words are called simple, contrasting complex words

which are composed of two or more morphemes (delet-ion, job-less-ness, in-

suffer-able).


Now, let’s look at another word, for example, the word wishful. How many

morphemes do you think are there in the word ‘wishful’?


Answer: The word ‘wishful’ is made up of two morphemes: wish having the

meaning of ‘a need, desire’ and -ful meaning ‘full of N’.

2.2. Free and Bound Morphemes

Free morphemes refer to morphemes that are words on their own and can

stand on their own. They do not have to attach to other words in a sentence. A

morpheme that must attach to another element in a sentence is called bound.

Take the word crying, for instance. Cry is a free morpheme since it can be used

as a word without anything attaching to it; however, the bound morpheme -ing

cannot stand on its own and must attach to cry.

Example: paint is a free morpheme while the morpheme -ness is bound.

It is important to note that there are languages that express concepts

differently from English. Where English refers to certain concepts using free

morphemes, other languages use bound morphemes (and vice versa).

2.2.3. Roots and Affixes

To understand the complex internal structures of words, we must first each

morpheme as well as identify its role in assigning meaning and function to the

larger word.


Complex words are made up of a root and one of more affixes. The root

contributes to the core meaning of the word. Roots usually belong to a lexical

category such as noun (N), verb (V), adjective (Adj), preposition (P), or adverb

(Adv).


Affixes, on the other hand, do not belong to lexical categories and are bound

morphemes. An example of an affix is -ness, a bound morpheme that combines

with adjectives such as fluffy and kind to produce nouns meaning ‘quality or

state of X’.

Correct: (b) – trust! ‘Trust’, as a free-standing word, contains the core meaning

of ‘distrusting’. It cannot be broken down any further and it belongs to a lexical

category (N). On the other hand, affixes dis- and -ing cannot stand alone and

do not belong to any lexical categories.

Bases

A form to which an affix attaches is called a base. In some cases, the base is

also the root. In words such as kindness, the affix -ness attaches to the base

kind, which happens to be the root. In larger, more complex words such as

joblessness, we attach -less to the first base which is the root job (notice how

the root carries the main meaning here). Then -ness attaches to the second

base which is no longer the root, but a unit made up of job and -less.



   Tip: All roots can be bases, but not all bases are roots!

2.3. Types of Affixes

Affixes can be classified based on their position with respect to the base. An

affix attaching to the front of the base is called a prefix while an affix attaching

to the end of its base is called a suffix.

3. Derivation and Inflection

As mentioned in the previous section, affixes can be categorized based on their

position to the base. Affixes are also classified with respect to their function.


Derivation refers to an affix that creates a word with a changed meaning

and/or lexical category. There is a multitude of derivational affixes in English

such as -er, -ment, -al, and -ize. For instance, the suffix -al attaches to nouns

such as condition to form an adjective meaning ‘the property/character of N’.

As such, condition (N) becomes condition-al (Adj) which is a new word with a

completely different lexical category. Once a new word is formed, it becomes a

lexical item that typically receives its own entry in the speaker’s mental

dictionary.


Inflection is the process by which a language marks changes in grammatical

information. Inflection does not change either the meaning or the lexical

category of the words. Inflection is also typically realized through affixation. In

English, there are only eight inflectional affixes all of which are suffixes. See the

Word Formation Processes

Compounding

Earlier in the chapter we have seen two of the most common word formation

processes in English, as well as other languages – inflection and derivation.

Compounding is another process by which we create new words in English.

Compounding involves joining together two existing words into a new unit.

Most of the time, the resulting compounds belong to one of the following three

lexical categories: a noun, an adjective, or a verb. A few examples include

parking ticket (N), force-feed (V), and carsick (Adj).


Notice that for most compounds in English, the rightmost element determines

the lexical category of the entire compound. A parking ticket is a noun since

the rightmost morpheme is a noun. The same applies to force-feed and carsick

– feed in this context is a verb while sick is an adjective. The element that

determines the lexical category of a compound is the head.


Additionally, the formation of compounds is not limited to two morphemes,

allowing multiple compounds to combine into larger words:


(1) Train station

(2) Train station exit

expansion of our dictionaries at incredibly short intervals. Here are a few

compounds the Marriam-Webster Dictionary added in March of 2018:

Properties of Compounds

Compounds are written in a multitude of ways, sometimes being represented

with a hyphen, as a single word or as separate words. The pronunciation of

compounds, however, is very different to that of separate words. Typically, the

stress falls on the first element of the compound whereas in non-compound

word combinations the stress usually falls on the second element. For

instance, the compound greenhouse, meaning a glass-enclosed garden,

receives the stress on the adjective green. Meanwhile, the expression green

house, meaning a house that is green in colour, stresses the noun house.

Endocentric and Exocentric Compounds

The compounds are additionally classified based on the meaning they convey

to the speaker. Endocentric compounds tend to relay meaning that is related

to the head of the compound. Thus, an earthworm is a type of worm and self-

care is a type of care.


In other cases, the meaning of the compound is not directly derived from the

second morpheme of the compound. Boldface is not a type of face, but a

typeface made with thick strokes. Similarly, a bluebell is not a type of bell but a

flower. These compounds are called exocentric.

Other Word Formation Processes

Internal Change

Internal change is one of the word formation processes related to inflection.

During internal change, one non-morphemic element is substituted for

another to mark grammatical changes. A common example of internal change

is mouse (singular) becoming mice (plural).


Ablaut is a form of internal change that deals with vowel alternations that

mark grammatical contrasts as seen in swim (present) - swam (past) and drink

(present) - drank (past).


Umlaut is a type of internal change that reflects earlier stages in a language’s

development. Two of the most famous examples of umlaut in English are the

plural forms geese and feet.

Suppletion

Suppletion is the process by which one morpheme is replaced by another

completely unrelated morpheme. For example, the English verb go is replaced

by went in the past tense.

Reduplication

Another common word formation process that can denote grammatical as well

as semantic contrast is called reduplication. Partial reduplication involves

repeating a part of the morphological base. Partial reduplication is seen in

Tagalog, which copies the first consonant-vowel cluster of the base:

morphological base to change meaning. Indonesian is a language that uses full

Zero Derivation

Zero derivation or conversion is a process that allows a language to assign a

new lexical category to an already existing word. Since zero derivation changes

the lexical category of the word, it is considered to by a type of derivation

process which does not involve affixation. Some of the more recent

conversions include Google (N) – to Google (V), inbox (N) – to inbox (V), and

Skype (N) – to Skype (V). Notice the way many of the recently created company

and product names (originally nouns) are converted into verbs.

Clipping

A process which shortens a multisyllabic word by deleting of one or more

syllables is called clipping. Names are a common product of this process,

resulting in names such as Rob, Kat, and Liz. Other examples of clipping

include fan from fanatic, bio from biology, and app from application.

Blending

Blending creates new words from parts of already existing words, typically

from the first part of the first element and the final part of the other element.

Some of the most common blends include brunch from breakfast and lunch,

motel from motor and hotel, and smog from smoke and fog. A more recent

addition to the dictionary includes the blend schnoodle, which is a cross

between a schnauzer and a poodle.

Backformation

Backformation is a process that creates new lexical entries by removing an

affix (real or supposed) from an already existing word. Donate was originally

formed from the noun donation by removing the supposed suffix -ion which

attaches to verbs in order to create nouns. In English, words ending in the suffix

-er or -or are also susceptible to backformation where nouns such as editor

become misanalysed to form verb forms like edit.

Acronyms and Initialisms

Acronyms are created by taking the first letters of the words in a phrase and

pronouncing them as a word. Examples of acronyms include NASA (National

Aeronautics and Space Administration), NATO (North Atlantic Treaty

Organization), and BOGO (buy one, get one).


Initialisms, also known as abbreviations, contrast acronyms as they are

pronounced as a string of letters rather than a complete word. Initialisms

include USA (United States of America), CD (Compact Disc), and FBI (Federal

Bureau of Investigation).

Coinage

Coinage is another word formation process which produces new words from

scratch. Coinage is responsible for product names, company names, and so on.

Words like Kleenex, Kodak, and Teflon are examples of product names created

without using any of the previously mentioned word formation processes.

Eponymy

Eponyms are new words created from names of people. Ferris wheel was

named after George Washington Gale Ferris Jr., jacuzzi named after Candido

Jacuzzi, and watt was named after a scientist James Watt.

Introduction

Semantics is the study of meaning in human language. Words become

phrases when spoken together, and they provide meaning through their

semantic relation.

Word relations

Synonyms are words or expressions that generally have the same meaning.

Examples include remember/recall and big/large. Antonyms on the other

hand are words that contain opposite meanings such as light/dark and

up/down. Polysemy is when words have two or more unrelated meanings. For

example, the word ‘mark’ has multiple meanings: an exam score (he got a good

mark), a spec on something (the pencil left a mark), or a target (she hit the

mark). Homophony is when words that sound the same have different

meanings. For example, the word ‘light’ can mean both not heavy and

illumination.

What is meaning?

Connotation

Connotation is the relationship between words that can be activated with a

word’s use. For example, the word winter activates words of ‘snow’, ‘cold’, ‘short

days’, ‘frozen fingertips’ and the like. These associations make the word’s

connotation, but it does not represent the meaning of the word.

Denotation

Denotation is the primary meaning of what a word represents and refers to.

The denotation of the word winter for example, corresponds to the season

between spring and fall, regardless of whether it is cold and unpleasant.

Denotation, however is not the same as meaning. This is because there are

words such as unicorn and phrases such as the present king of France, which

have no referents in the real world, but still have meaning.

Introduction

Humans can communicate by using a wide range of modalities (gestures, sign

natural languages. Humans spoke long before they started to write, and this is

reflected in their anatomical specialization. Moreover, humans also appear to

have specialized neural mechanisms for the perception of speech sounds.


Speech is the primary way in which we as humans communicate using

Coarticulation


So far, we’ve looked at speech sounds in isolation; however, speech is not a

series of isolated occurrences. We have introduced the basic parameters for

the classification of vowels and consonants, but speech production is not a

series of isolated events. speech is a very complex phenomenon. As

articulatory organs operate independently of each other, many adjustments

have to be made in order to produce fast speech.


Quite often, more than one articulator is active in order to allow the

production of fast speech. For example, when we produce a sound sequence

like [pl], we do not articulate the sound [p], then stop, and then articulate

sound [l]. Rather, as the sequence [pl] is produced, the tongue tip will start to

move toward the alveolar ridge before the lips separate. As a result of these

processes, the articulation of one sound affects the surrounding sounds. When

we produce sequences of speech segments, we coordinate muscular

movements to make smooth articulatory transitions. This is known as

coarticulation. as a result of more than one articulator being active,

articulatory adjustments take place. Such adjustments are called (articulatory)

processes. They change the nature of individual segments

Processes


Processes are the articulatory movements that occur during connected

speech.

Can make speech more efficient. For example, when we say tank, we

      don’t wait until we reach n to lower the velum. We start lowering it

      sooner, during the a articulation

     Can also make speech less efficient, but more distinct. For example,

     when people ask you to repeat what you said, you are likely to lengthen

      segments to make what you said easier to perceive (I said my name is

      Paauuul!)


Some common articulatory processes

Assimilation

Assimilation occurs when one phone becomes more similar in sound to a

nearby phone. Assimilation results from a sound becoming more like another

nearby sound in terms of one or more of its phonetic properties.


We have different types of assimilation. For instance, vowels become nasalized

([~]) when they come before a nasal consonant like in [tæ̃n] ‘tan’. This is an

example of Regressive assimilation, because the nasalization applies to a

prior segment (moving backwards). Progressive assimilation occurs when

the assimilation applies to a following segment (moving forwards), like in Scots
Gaelic [mũ] ‘about’.


Devoicing occurs when vowels or voiced consonants become voiceless. For

example, liquids and glides become devoiced after voiceless stops (ex. [p̥l̥æn]

‘plan’, [tr̥ɪk] ‘trick’).


Voicing occurs when voiceless segments become voiced. In Dutch, voiceless

fricatives become voiced before a voiced stop (ex. the f in afbellen ‘to cancel’ is

produced with [v]). Devoicing and voicing are types of voicing assimilation.

Introduction


In the previous chapter we have seen that humans are capable of perceiving

and producing a variety of speech sounds. While no language uses all of these

building blocks, every language selects its own set of sounds and organizes

them into an impressive system of patterns and constraints. Phonology deals

with such organized systems of abstract representations of sounds.


Phonology deals with the relationships between individual sounds, as well as

their organization on higher tiers (syllables). Additionally, phonology deals

with building blocks of speech sounds – the acoustic and articulatory features

that help us understand how and why sounds pattern in certain ways.

A speaker of any language produces a variety of speech sounds as they use

Phonemes and Allophones

Phonologists pay close attention to the speech sounds that are contrastive in

any given language. A phoneme is an abstract unit of sound that changes

meaning between words. Variations of the same phoneme are called

allophones, which are different pronunciations that do not change meaning.

Thus, in English the sounds [ĩ] and [i] are allophones of some common

phoneme since we do not use them contrastively (i.e they do not change

meaning). On the other hand, the vowels [æ] and [i] are clearly contrastive (see
back    and beak) and must be assigned to separate phonemes.


In terms of representation, linguists select the most widely used allophone

(depending on the conditions) to represent the phoneme written between

slashes. The allophones of any phoneme will be written in square brackets.


As you can see, the two variations of the vowel /i/ are allophones of the same

phoneme that don’t change meaning. At the same time, the two vowels /i/ and

/æ/ are separate phonemes and cannot be represented under the same tree.

Recall that phonemes are abstract, mental categories that exist in a speaker’s

mind. Allophones are the physical sounds that occur specifically because of

human capacity to speak. Allophones occur because of changes in articulation

(i.e. adjustments to your vocal tract) as we speak.

Building Syllable Structure

There is a simple way of identifying syllables and constructing them.


Step 1: Identifying the nucleus


The nucleus is an obligatory part of the syllable. Recall that nuclei tend to be

vowels, simply find all of the vowels in a transcribed word. To represent a

nucleus, write an N over the vowel. Above the nucleus symbol, indicate an R

which stands for rhyme. Rhymes are typically comprised of nuclei and codas if

consonants are present. Place a sigma above the rhyme to represent a syllable.

Step 2: Identifying the onset


Identify the longest cluster of consonants to the left of every nucleus. When

dealing with onsets, Maximize Onset Principle states that a we should make

the onset as long as possible, so long as the cluster conforms to the rules of the

particular language. As we are dealing with English, remember that there can

only be three consonants in the onset if the fist consonant is /s/. As you are

working through consonant clusters, keep in mind that if a cluster cannot

appear at the beginning of an English word, then it will not form an onset. To

show an onset in the structure, link the consonant to an O symbol which

should join the sigma symbol.





Step 3: Identifying the coda


Finally, any left-over consonants to the right of the nucleus form a coda. Link

them to the C symbol and attach the coda to the rhyme. If a syllable contains a

coda, it is considered a closed syllable, while a syllable without a coda is

called open.
